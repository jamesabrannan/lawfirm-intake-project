Yes. You can use free public sources to look up a Maryland criminal charge, read the applicable law and statutory penalties, and download appellate opinions interpreting it. For a specific prosecution, Maryland also provides a public docket search and access to many case records through the court clerk.

Maryland laws and penalties

What you need Public source
Statute text The Maryland General Assembly lets you browse the Maryland Code by article and read sections in HTML or download them as PDFs. Criminal Law is a starting point. Charges can also appear in other articles, such as Transportation. Maryland Code statutes
Law in effect on the alleged offense date Use the statute’s amendment history and the enacted chapter laws to check the applicable wording and effective dates. The State Law Library explains how to locate older versions. Maryland Code research guide · Legislative history guide
Sentencing Start with the exact subsection and any cross-referenced penalty provisions for imprisonment, fines, and mandatory minimums. The state sentencing commission also publishes a downloadable offense table, manual, and scoring matrices. Its guidelines provide recommended ranges for eligible circuit court cases. Maryland sentencing guidelines and offense table · Guidelines scope
Criminal procedure The State Law Library links to the Maryland Rules and other sources of Maryland law. Title 4 covers criminal causes. Maryland Code and Rules sources

Court opinions and case records

  • Reported appellate opinions. Maryland Courts provides downloadable opinions from the Supreme Court of Maryland and the Appellate Court of Maryland, with years back to 1995 in its search interface. Older cases also appear under the courts’ former names, the Court of Appeals and the Court of Special Appeals. Reported opinions · State Law Library case-law guide
  • Unreported appellate opinions. Maryland Courts posts full text from May 1, 2015 onward and directs readers to the clerk for older copies. Rule 1-104 limits when these opinions can be cited. Eligible opinions issued on or after July 1, 2023 may be cited for persuasive value when reported authority does not adequately address the issue. Unreported opinions and citation guidance
  • Records for a specific prosecution. Maryland Judiciary Case Search provides public docket information. For a charging document, order, or other filing, check with the clerk of the court handling the case. Access can depend on the document and any applicable restrictions. Maryland Case Search · Access to Court Records
  • Full-text search and bulk downloads. CourtListener offers free opinion search and bulk data files. Its jurisdiction list identifies the Maryland appellate collections. Harvard’s Caselaw Access Project provides historical opinions published in official reporters. CourtListener · Maryland coverage · Bulk downloads · Library of Congress guide to the Caselaw Access Project

How this works for an actual charge

Start with the exact article, section, subsection, and alleged offense date for each count on the charging document. Pull the version that applies, check the penalty provisions, then search reported opinions by statute number, offense name, and the specific legal issue. Follow later citing cases before relying on a decision.

For example, Maryland Criminal Law § 3-203 covers second-degree assault. The published statute lists up to 10 years and a $2,500 fine under subsection (b), and up to 10 years and a $5,000 fine for the felony variant under subsection (c). The reported opinion Nicolas v. State, 426 Md. 385 (2012), discusses the elements of different forms of second-degree assault. That gives you two public documents answering different questions about the same charge. Statute text · Nicolas v. State opinion PDF

These examples are publicly accessible.

Case What happened Relevance to the demo
Geronimo Lozano — New Jersey, charges dismissed in 2016 A later federal opinion records that DUI and breath-test refusal charges were dismissed after medical records substantiated his inability to provide sufficient breath samples and orthopedic disabilities that prevented field sobriety tests. A strong example of documented physical limitations undermining evidence used to charge someone. The public source is the subsequent civil lawsuit’s opinion. Federal opinion, page 5
Dr. Hilary Chong Wah — Queensland, Australia, 2005 She was acquitted of dangerous operation of a vehicle causing grievous bodily harm after a diabetic episode and blackout. According to ABC, medical testimony established that low blood sugar affected her reasoning and prevented her from recognizing the episode. A close factual example for researching sudden medical incapacity. Its jurisdiction and offense differ from Mateo’s. ABC report
Richard Lapointe — Connecticut, convictions overturned and charges dismissed in 2015 His lawyers argued that cognitive disabilities associated with Dandy-Walker syndrome made him vulnerable to a false confession. The successful appellate ruling concerned withheld evidence supporting an alibi. Prosecutors subsequently dropped the charges after further DNA testing. Supports a separate research question about disability, interview methods, and the reliability of admissions. Court opinion · AP account of the outcome
Plummer v. State — Maryland, 1997 The appellate court reversed automobile-manslaughter and reckless-driving convictions for insufficient evidence. Other traffic convictions remained for sentencing. Directly relevant Maryland authority for examining whether the evidence establishes reckless driving. Official opinion PDF
Tim Otto — Michigan, 2023 appellate decision The court vacated a reckless-driving-causing-death conviction arising from brake failure. The decision addressed the statute’s reach and counsel’s failure to challenge the charge. Illustrates how mechanical evidence and statutory interpretation can defeat a conviction. The cited report describes the appellate outcome at that time. AP report

There is also a useful Maryland medical-incapacity opinion, Cooper v. Singleton (2014). A driver successfully presented sudden incapacity to a jury in a civil negligence case. The opinion discusses whether the incapacity was sudden and unforeseeable, including the significance of prior warning. It supplies Maryland research leads, though its civil standards require careful treatment when researching a criminal charge. Official opinion PDF
